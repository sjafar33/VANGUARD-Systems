# Moonbase Academy written submission

## Project Name

Moonbase Academy

## High-Level Project Summary

Moonbase Academy is an offline iPhone and iPad game for children ages 7–12 that turns a lunar outpost into a repeatable science experiment. Players choose equipment and operating settings, predict an outcome, explain their reasoning, test the plan and use evidence to revise a decision.

Four drills explore power, life support, food production, storm sheltering and communication delay. Choices have costs: another empty battery cannot create energy, while reducing air-maker power saves electricity but consumes stored oxygen. Ready-to-use example labs let reviewers explore these trade-offs without unlocking buildings or changing a saved base.

NASA crew-resource references, Veggie information, archived solar-particle-event timestamps and the average Earth–Moon distance inform the model. Reference data, derived calculations and simplified training rules are identified separately. The working native prototype aims to help young learners explain why a mission succeeds or fails, with offline play and opportunities to try another strategy.

## Detailed Project Description

The problem and our approach
Space facts alone do not explain why keeping astronauts alive requires difficult choices. Moonbase Academy addresses the Junior Astronaut Mission Trainer challenge by giving children ages 7–12 control over a fictional lunar outpost. Limited electricity, oxygen storage, cargo and time turn mission planning into decisions whose consequences can be observed.

How a player investigates
The player builds a base, chooses a mission plan, predicts its outcome and selects a reason. A deterministic simulation then tests the equipment and settings. Results distinguish an accurate forecast from a successful mission: correctly predicting failure earns recognition, but the engineering problem still needs a solution. The player can inspect the explanation, revise a choice and test again. Predictions and reasons never change the simulation physics.

Four connected engineering lessons
Lights Out explores solar generation, battery storage and life support through sunlight followed by a six-hour training shadow. The Living Base uses a 33-day crop reference to explore how greenhouse lighting, oxygen production and optional research share a daily energy budget. Storm Drill introduces shelter and resource planning over archived particle-event durations. Signal Lag explores how a rover continues moving while a camera picture travels to Earth and a stop command travels back; an onboard computer avoids that delay but occupies cargo space that could carry a camera.

A measurable choice with a visible cost
In the starter shadow example, one solar array and one battery store 2.4 kWh during six hours of sunlight at Full air. The following six hours of shadow require 3.6 kWh, so the test stops at mission hour 10. Adding an empty battery does not fix insufficient generation. Keeping the equipment unchanged and setting only shadow air to Half completes the 12-hour test. Compared with Full air over the shadow interval, this saves 1.5 kWh while withdrawing approximately 0.1975 kg of oxygen from storage. These are simulation outputs using NASA crew demand and explicitly authored equipment assumptions, not spacecraft performance specifications.

Connecting experiments to the base
Test My Base counts the player’s working solar arrays and batteries and uses the same resource engine as the reference drill. A separate own-base storm rehearsal uses the working shelter and charged batteries. Its pinned archived event lasts 98 hours 5 minutes; success requires shelter, a minimum oxygen reserve and completion of a science task. Sheltering alone does not complete that task. Preparation energy and backup power are labeled training rules. Example labs provide ready-made configurations without awarding campaign rewards or overwriting saved progress.

Implementation and technical evidence
The native prototype uses Swift, SwiftUI, SpriteKit, Core Graphics, Foundation and XCTest. Procedural artwork and synthesized sound are generated by project code. Bundled science inputs allow the app to work offline without accounts, ads, analytics or a backend. Unit and simulator UI checks cover resource constraints, source sensitivity, example-save isolation, predictions and selected interaction flows. Changing test copies of NASA-derived inputs changes outcomes: greater crew oxygen demand can break a reserve plan, a longer crop reference can prevent harvest, and a greater Earth–Moon distance moves the rover’s stopping point. Technical tests establish behavior; they do not establish educational effectiveness.

Intended impact and prototype limits
The intended use is guided exploration at home or in a classroom: predict which change might help, test it and explain the evidence. Optional read-aloud, reduced motion and large-text layouts support different ways of engaging. No classroom study or measured learning improvement is claimed. The current project is a native prototype; browser play and App Store distribution are not provided. Water, carbon dioxide, thermal control, complete nutrition, radiation transport and realistic rover braking are outside scope. Further work includes learner testing and refinement of guidance and accessibility. NASA does not endorse this project.

## NASA Data

NASA information changes resource consumption, crop timing, storm duration and rover behavior. The app embeds archived/reference information; it makes no live API calls. NASA references, derived values, training assumptions and physical constants are distinguished in the app.

NASA Life Support Baseline Values and Assumptions Document
https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20210024855
NASA/TP-2015-218570/REV2, February 2022, Table 3-31 supplies nominal adult crew references of 0.895 kg oxygen and 0.800 kg dry food per person per day. The model scales consumption with crew count and elapsed simulated time. These are adult design-study references, not children’s needs or exact requirements for every astronaut. Air-maker output, efficiency and tank capacity remain training assumptions.

NASA Veggie fact sheet
https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/veggie_fact_sheet_508.pdf
The VEG-01 growth dates provide a 33-day lettuce reference; the fact sheet also describes approximately 70 W operating power for lights, fans and control electronics. These inform the garden horizon and operating-power reference. Applying ISS information to a fictional lunar greenhouse is a teaching adaptation. Lighting controls, growth response and the daily energy allowance are simplified rules. Lettuce supplements packed food; it does not provide a complete crew diet.

NASA CCMC SEP Scoreboard archive
https://ccmc.gsfc.nasa.gov/challenges/sep/events/
Archived GOES-13 observations above 10 MeV, using the archive’s 10 pfu threshold, supply storm timestamps. The September 10, 2017 event runs from 16:45 UTC to September 14 at 18:50 UTC, with its peak on September 11 at 11:45 UTC. Timestamp differences give 19 hours rising and 79 hours 5 minutes fading. Eight archived events support reference-drill replay variation. These satellite observations near Earth provide a rehearsal clock; they do not determine radiation dose or health risk on the Moon. DONKI is not used.

NASA Moon Facts
https://science.nasa.gov/moon/facts/
The 384,400 km average Earth–Moon distance combines with the SI speed of light to produce approximately 1.282 seconds one way and 2.564 seconds for a picture-and-command round trip. This delay affects where the rover stops. The model assumes constant distance, uniform rover speed and instantaneous braking when the command arrives.

Training assumptions
Examples include a 1 kW solar array, a 4 kWh battery, two adult crew, simplified air-maker behavior, a six-hour training shadow, cargo costs and fictional preparation/backup power. These are teaching rules, not validated spacecraft specifications.
